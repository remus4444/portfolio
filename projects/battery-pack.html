The problem

When Formula Buckeyes switched from internal combustion to electric, the battery pack was run primarily as an electrical engineering project. That focus produced strong electronics, but the mechanical design hadn't received the same attention. The pack didn't meet several FSAE rules, was expensive to produce, and couldn't be assembled safely. As a result, the team went its first two electric seasons without a functioning battery pack.

What I did

Rather than starting from scratch, I kept the pack's core concept and treated three of its existing elements as fixed constraints for the rest of the design:

  • The module layout
  • Passive cooling
  • The expensive, sponsor-manufactured main containment case

Within those constraints:

  • Led the mechanical redesign with a team of assigned mechanical engineers, tracking work in Notion.
  • Reworked portions of the six 96 V subsegments to allow secure connections and voltage tapping from one subsegment to the next.
  • Designed portions of the top-box, the section where the control electronics are stored, helping to shrink packaging footprints and improve manufacturability.

Subsystem showcases

Electronics box

The pack's electronics used to be spread across the top-box. I consolidated them into a single main housing, the electronics box, which shrank the overall PCB footprint by a factor of three. The freed space in the top-box went to larger maintenance plugs that meet the FSAE rules. The electronics box holds five boards:

  • Precharge discharge board: controls the contactors so the motor controllers are charged and discharged gradually, avoiding a short-circuit-like current surge when the pack is switched on or off.
  • Precharge discharge daughter board: takes commands from the precharge discharge board and uses relays to physically switch the contactors.
  • High voltage detection board: detects the presence of high voltage and alerts other subsystems, such as the safety board.
  • High voltage indicator board: detects the presence of high voltage and lights an LED, visible on the lid, to warn people once the pack is assembled.
  • Insulation measurement device: monitors the insulation between the high-voltage system and the chassis, and shuts down the pack if it detects a break.

Much of the work on the electronics box was coordination with the electronics subteam. Together we made sure every connector had enough vertical space to be installed and for its wires to bend, and that the wiring routed through the box efficiently.

I also wrote a 20-page build guide for the box so that any team member could assemble it easily.

Results

After many long hours, the team and I closed a design cycle that had been open for two years in just three months, and assembled Formula Buckeyes' first functioning battery pack.

Its performance numbers are modest. The cell choice and module layout were inherited, and changing them was outside the scope of this redesign. The goal was a pack the team could build, run and learn from, and that is what this one is.

What I'd do differently

I'd keep design reviews on the schedule even when the timeline got tight. As our schedule compressed, reviews were the first thing to go. Spending an extra hour on each subassembly would have caught the small errors that later forced an unnecessary revision.

I'd also stop printing parts directly in our production material, PC-FR, which is expensive and hard to get. Instead I'd print test-fit prototypes in PLA first and move to PC-FR only once the fit was confirmed.
